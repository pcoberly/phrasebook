Which Arabic

This is Egyptian Arabic, the everyday Arabic of Cairo. Thanks to Egyptian films and television it is the most widely understood spoken Arabic, from Morocco to the Gulf. Newspapers and the news use Modern Standard Arabic, which nobody speaks at home. It is written from right to left, and everyday writing leaves out the short vowels, so the "sounds like" spellings matter more than usual. They put the stressed syllable in capitals, and a doubled vowel (aa, ee, uu) is a long one.

How the letters join

Most letters join to the next one and change shape depending on where they sit in a word, so the same letter can look quite different. ال (il) means the, and is written joined to its word: المطار (il-ma-TAAR), the airport. Before many letters its l blends into the next sound: السلام is said is-sa-LAAM.

Man or woman

Some words change with the speaker's gender. A man says مش فاهم (mish FAA-him), I don't understand, and a woman says مش فاهمة (mish FAH-ma). Where a phrase differs, the woman's form is shown under it as "A woman says", and the female voice says that form.

"You" changes with the gender of the person you speak to, too, often only in how the end is said: إزيك is iz-ZAY-yak to a man and iz-ZAY-yik to a woman. The notes say where. Many questions here use forms that are the same for anyone, such as ممكن (MUM-kin), "is it possible".

Being polite

Greet before you ask: السلام عليكم works anywhere, with anyone. To a stranger or an older person, use حضرتك (had-RIT-ak), the polite you. Add لو سمحت (law sa-MAHT) to a request. You will hear إن شاء الله (in SHAA al-LAAH), God willing, about anything in the future.

Numbers

Egypt mostly uses the Arabic digits you know on prices and in shops, but you will also see the Eastern Arabic digits, written left to right: ٠ ١ ٢ ٣ ٤ ٥ ٦ ٧ ٨ ٩ are 0 to 9. A price of ٥٠ is 50.
