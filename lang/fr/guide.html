Which French

This is French as spoken in France, in the polite vous form, which suits shop staff, waiters and strangers. The "sounds like" spellings put the stressed syllable in capitals. French stresses the last syllable of a phrase, and the syllables before it are nearly even.

Always say bonjour

Greet people with bonjour when you walk into a shop, a café or an office, and say au revoir when you leave. Skipping it comes across as rude, and saying it makes people far more friendly and willing to help.

Nasal vowels

Air goes through the nose and the n or m is not sounded. They are the hardest part for English speakers, so listen to the audio.

Silent letters and liaison

Most final consonants are silent: petit, vous, trop. The exceptions are often c, r, f and l (as in "careful"): sac, cher, neuf, sel. Before a word starting with a vowel a silent final consonant may come back and link the two words, so vous avez sounds like "voo-zah-VAY".

You, polite and casual

Vous is polite, and is the safe choice with anyone you don't know. Tu is for friends, family and children. The phrases here use vous.

Masculine and feminine

Every noun is masculine or feminine: le café, la gare, un billet, une table. Adjectives follow: je suis allergique is the same for a man or a woman, but content (a man) and contente (a woman) differ by an ending you can't hear.

Numbers

One to ten are the ones to learn first. Seventy is soixante-dix (sixty-ten) and ninety is quatre-vingt-dix (four-twenty-ten) in France, while Belgium and Switzerland say septante and nonante.
