Danish

Danish is spoken by about six million people in Denmark, and understood in the Faroe Islands and Greenland. Swedes and Norwegians read it easily but often struggle to understand it spoken: Danes swallow many of their consonants. Nearly every Dane speaks excellent English, so these phrases are about the smile. The "sounds like" spellings put the stressed syllable in capitals.

Silent and soft letters

Many written letters are soft or silent. A d after a vowel is usually a soft dh, close to the th in "this": hedder (HEDH-er). The d in godt and the h in hv are silent: hvad is said va. A g after a vowel often fades away, and r is a soft sound in the throat, or disappears into the vowel.

The stød

Danish has a little catch in the voice, the stød, that can change a word's meaning. You don't need to master it to be understood: just copy the audio.

Being polite

Danes are informal: everyone is du, you, and first names are normal. There is no common word for please; politeness lives in tak, thanks, which Danes say constantly, and in a friendly tone. Wait for the green light before crossing, even on an empty street, and keep out of the bike lanes.

Numbers

Danish tens from 50 to 90 come from an old system of counting in twenties: 50 is halvtreds, 60 tres, 70 halvfjerds, 80 firs and 90 halvfems. Even Danes smile about it.
