Which Portuguese

This is the Portuguese of Portugal, as spoken in Lisbon. Brazil's Portuguese is written much the same, but Portugal swallows its unstressed vowels, so it sounds quicker and softer, almost Slavic at times. Some everyday words differ too: a train is a comboio, not a trem. The "sounds like" spellings put the stressed syllable in capitals.

Swallowed vowels

Unstressed vowels shrink or vanish. An unstressed e at the end is almost silent: sete (SET), noite (NOYT). An unstressed a is a short uh: casa (KAH-zuh). An unstressed o is oo: obrigado (oh-bree-GAH-doo). Listen to the audio and don't try to say every letter.

Nasal vowels

A vowel with a tilde (ã, õ), or before m or n at the end of a syllable, is said partly through the nose, as in French: não (nowng), bem (bayng), sim (seeng). The spellings write it with ng, but don't fully say the g.

Man or woman

A few words change with the speaker's gender. A man says obrigado, thank you, and a woman says obrigada, whoever they are thanking. Where a phrase differs, the woman's form is shown under it as "A woman says", and the female voice says that form.

Being polite

The Portuguese are more formal than Brazilians. With strangers, leave out "you" altogether and use the verb on its own: Fala inglês?, do you speak English?, Pode repetir?, can you repeat? Você, the everyday Brazilian you, can sound abrupt here. Say bom dia when you walk into a shop or café, and obrigado as you leave.
