Which English

This is American English, with American voices. British, Australian and other Englishes sound different and use some different words, and the notes say where: the check is the bill in Britain, and the restroom is the toilets or the loo. Everyone understands both.

Spelling and sound

English spelling is famously unreliable: the same letters can sound different (though, through, tough) and the same sound can be spelled many ways. Trust the audio and the "sounds like" spellings rather than the letters. They put the stressed syllable in capitals.

Sounds to practise

LettersSound like
thThe tongue tip between the teeth: soft in thank you, buzzing in this
rThe tongue curls back and never touches the roof of the mouth. Americans say it everywhere; the British drop it at the end of a syllable
wRounded lips, like a quick oo: water
hAlways breathed at the start of a word: hello, help
t between vowelsIn American English, a quick d: water sounds like WAH-der

Stress and rhythm

English stresses one syllable strongly and swallows the others, so small words like a, the and to are quick and weak: a table for two sounds like a-TAY-bul-fer-TOO. Getting the stress right matters more than every vowel.

Being polite

English softens requests: Could I...?, Can I get...? and I'd like... are polite; I want sounds blunt. Say please and thank you often, and sorry when you bump into someone. English has only one "you", the same for anyone.

Tipping and money

In the US, tip 18 to 20% in restaurants and bars, and sales tax is added at the register. In Britain and Australia, tipping is smaller or optional. Numbers use a comma for thousands and a dot for decimals: $1,250.50.
