Which Zulu

Zulu, isiZulu, is South Africa's most spoken home language, above all in KwaZulu-Natal (Durban) and Johannesburg. Almost everyone also speaks English, which Zulu borrows from freely, so the point of these phrases is the smile: a greeting in Zulu is noticed and appreciated.

Reading Zulu

Zulu is written as it sounds. The five vowels are pure, as in Spanish or Italian: a as in "father", e as in "bed", i as in "feet", o as in "more", u as in "food". The "sounds like" spellings put the stressed syllable in capitals, almost always the second to last.

The clicks

Zulu has three click sounds, borrowed long ago from the Khoisan languages. The spellings show them as [c], [q] and [x]. Make the click, then go straight on to the vowel. Listen to the audio; nobody minds if your clicks are soft.

LetterHow to click
c [c]The tongue tip pulls back from the top front teeth, like a disapproving "tsk": cha, no
q [q]The tongue pulls down sharply from the roof of the mouth, a loud pop like a cork: angiqondi, I don't understand
x [x]The side of the tongue pulls away from the side teeth, the sound for geeing up a horse: uxolo, sorry

Written gc, gq, gx, the click is voiced and followed by a g sound; nc, nq, nx add a hum through the nose before it.

How words work

Zulu builds words from pieces at the front: ngi- means I, u- you, ku- it. So ngiyabonga is "I thank", and kuyashisa is "it is hot". Place names take e- for "to" or "at": eThekwini, to Durban. Nouns change their front too: ithikithi, a ticket, amathikithi, tickets.

Being polite

Always greet before anything else, with Sawubona, and wait for the reply. Show respect to older people: greet them first, and use the plural Sanibonani and Ninjani? as a mark of respect. Give and receive with the right hand, or both hands for something offered with care. Ngicela, I ask, makes any request polite.
