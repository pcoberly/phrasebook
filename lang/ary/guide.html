Which Arabic

This is Moroccan Arabic, Darija (DAA-ree-zha), what people actually speak in Morocco. It is quite different from the Arabic of Egypt or the Middle East: it drops many vowels, so words sound short and clipped, and it borrows freely from French, Spanish, and Amazigh (Berber). Moroccans understand Egyptian Arabic from television, but other Arabic speakers often struggle with Darija. Many Moroccans also speak Amazigh, and French is widely spoken, above all in cities, hotels, and offices.

Darija has no standard spelling and is mostly spoken, not written, so it is written here in Arabic letters as people text it. Read the "sounds like" spellings: they put the stressed syllable in capitals, a doubled vowel (aa, ee, oo) is long, and a hyphen often joins consonants with no vowel between them: b-ZAAF.

Man or woman

A few words change with the speaker's gender. A man says محتاج طبيب (meh-TAAZH t-BEEB), I need a doctor, and a woman says محتاجة طبيب (meh-TAA-zha t-BEEB). Where a phrase differs, the woman's form is shown under it as "A woman says", and the female voice says that form. Darija changes less than Egyptian Arabic: "I" verbs and "your" are the same for everyone.

Speaking to someone, "you" changes for a woman in some words, such as عافاك, please: a-FAAK to a man and a-FAA-kee to a woman. The notes say where.

Being polite

Greet first: السلام عليكم works anywhere. Add عافاك (a-FAAK) to any request. Hospitality is a big deal: accept a glass of mint tea if you can, and eat with your right hand. In the souks, sellers can be insistent: a smiling لا، شكرا and walking on is perfectly polite. You will hear إن شاء الله (in-SHAA l-LAAH), God willing, about anything in the future, and واخا (WAA-kha), OK, all day long.

French words

Darija is full of French: la gare (la-GAAR), the station, le menu, la carte, les toilettes. If you speak some French, use it; if you don't, these borrowed words are easy wins.
