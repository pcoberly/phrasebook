Which Korean

This is the standard Korean of Seoul, in the polite style that ends in -yo (요), which is right with strangers, staff and shopkeepers. 합니다 (ham-ni-da) endings, as in 감사합니다, are a little more formal. Korean doesn't stress syllables the way English does: give each one about the same weight, so the "sounds like" spellings have no capitals.

Hangul

Korean is written in Hangul, an alphabet of 24 letters grouped into square blocks, one block per syllable. 한 is ㅎ h + ㅏ a + ㄴ n: han. A block that starts with a vowel sound gets a silent ㅇ in front.

Vowels

ㅏa, as in "father"
ㅓuh, as in "up" but rounder: 어디 (uh-di)
ㅗo, as in "go" but short
ㅜoo, as in "food"
ㅡeu: oo with the lips spread flat, as if smiling
ㅣee, as in "feet"
ㅐ, ㅔe, as in "bed". Most Seoul speakers say them the same

A short stroke added to a vowel puts a y in front: ㅑ ya, ㅕ yuh, ㅛ yo, ㅠ yu.

Sounds that change

Letters change to flow into the next one, and the "sounds like" spellings follow how it is said: 십만 is written ship + man but said shim-man, and 감사합니다 is said gam-sa-ham-ni-da.

Two sets of numbers

The numbers here (일 il, 이 ee, 삼 sam) are the ones for prices, money, dates and phone numbers. Korean has a second, native set for counting things, people and hours, and for your age: 하나 (ha-na), 둘 (dool), 셋 (set), 넷 (net), 다섯 (da-suht). Big numbers count in 만 (man), ten thousands.
